Understanding the Crisis

On August 15, 2021, the Taliban took over Afghanistan. Despite initial promises to protect women's rights in the country, the Taliban implemented restrictive policies that effectively erased women from public life.

Photo by Atef Aryan/AFP.

Women's Rights in Crisis

Decades of progress in women's rights were erased as the Taliban enforced strict rules that limited women's freedom. Some restrictions that women face are:

  • Women are required to cover their faces in public.
  • Women are not allowed to speak in public under the "vice and virtue" law.
  • Women must be accompanied by a mahram (male chaperone) when accessing public services, transportation, or when travelling more than 78 kilometers.
  • Women are banned from working outside of the home, except for certain sectors such as primary education and healthcare (although medical education has been restricted by the Taliban).

Health

Basic health services are available to just 10% of Afghan women. Access to contraception is restricted, leading to concerns over women's sexual and reproductive health. Women's mental health has plummeted. A 2024 UN Women report based on consultations with thousands of Afghan women found that 68% of women reported having "bad" or "very bad" mental health. 98% felt they had "limited or no" influence over decisions made in communities.

Education

Afghanistan is the only country in the world where girls are banned from going to secondary school and beyond. As of August 2024, 1.1 million girls are out of school, and 100,000 women are out of university.

Legal Rights

Representation of women in government was erased as the Taliban abolished the Ministry of Women's Affairs and barred women from running for government positions. Freedom of peaceful assembly is heavily restricted. Afghan Witness, a research team, reported that 94% of all women's protests took place indoors. Additionally, the Taliban have enforced arbitrary arrests, disappearances, and unlawful detention.

Personal Stories

Note: all names, locations, and course of events have been changed to protect the privacy and safety of the women featured.

Nasima

Peacebuilder and Women's Rights Activist

Before the Taliban takeover, Nasima was the leader of a civil society organization elevating the voices of Afghan women. She was running 2 businesses and was working on her master's degree. She has continued to advocate for the rights of women despite facing challenges from the Taliban.


"Something died inside me on 15 August 2021, or at least that is how I felt: my hopes crushed, my education irrelevant, my investment in Afghanistan gone."

Zarina

Entrepreneur

Zarina had planned on expanding her bakery business. After the Taliban takeover, she could no longer expand her business, and her employees lost their jobs. However, Zarina hopes to keep running her business, making it a safe space for women in her province.


"I am heartbroken, but hopeful [...] My shop will again be a place for women to come together."


Read about Zarina's story here.

Najiba

Former University Lecturer and Counselor

Najiba has been a counselor helping women survivors of violence for the past 20 years. Since the Taliban takeover, Najiba has helped increasing numbers of women and girls. She is committed to helping the women in her community by providing counseling and enrolling them in literacy and vocational training classes.


"I am a healer—for the women I work with, for my community. I am helping women overcome trauma but most importantly, I am helping them find the hope they lost and make new, brighter plans."
